## Definition

A **field** is a commutative ring with identity $1 \neq 0$ in which every nonzero
element has a multiplicative inverse. Concretely: $(F, +)$ is an abelian group,
$(F \setminus \{0\}, \cdot)$ is an abelian group, and multiplication distributes
over addition. $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/p$ for
prime $p$ are fields; $\mathbb{Z}$ is not, because $2$ has no inverse.

**Field theory** is the study of fields and, far more than of any single field,
of **extensions**: a field $L$ containing a field $K$ as a subfield, written
$L/K$. Two invariants organise everything. The **characteristic** of $F$ is the
non-negative generator of the kernel of the unique ring homomorphism
$\mathbb{Z} \to F$; it is either $0$ or a prime $p$, since the image is an
integral domain. And the **degree** $[L:K]$ is the dimension of $L$ as a
$K$-vector space. The whole subject runs on the observation that the second of
these is a number you can compute with.

## Why it matters

Field theory converts questions about roots of polynomials — which have no
obvious handle — into questions about dimension, which have every handle linear
algebra offers. That conversion settles problems that resisted direct attack for
two thousand years. A length constructible by straightedge and compass lies in a
tower of degree-$2$ extensions of $\mathbb{Q}$, so its degree over $\mathbb{Q}$
is a power of $2$; the edge of a doubled cube satisfies $x^3 - 2$ and has degree
$3$; $3$ does not divide any power of $2$; the cube cannot be doubled. The whole
argument is the tower law and a divisibility check.

It also fixes what linear algebra means. Every vector space, matrix and
eigenvalue lives over some field, and which theorems hold depends on which one:
a real matrix need not have an eigenvalue, the same matrix over $\mathbb{C}$
always does. And finite fields are not a curiosity — they are the arithmetic
inside AES, Reed–Solomon codes and elliptic-curve cryptography.

## Intuition

Extending a field is wishing a root into existence and then keeping honest books
about it. You want $\sqrt{2}$; you write down all expressions $a + b\sqrt{2}$
with $a, b \in \mathbb{Q}$; you declare that wherever $(\sqrt{2})^2$ appears it
becomes $2$. The result is closed under the four operations and has
$\mathbb{Q}$-basis $\{1, \sqrt{2}\}$ — degree $2$.

It is clock arithmetic one level up. $\mathbb{Z}/n$ is a field exactly when $n$
is prime; $K[x]/(f)$ is a field exactly when $f$ is irreducible. In both cases
invertibility comes from Bézout: if $\gcd(g, f) = 1$ then $ug + vf = 1$, so $u$
inverts $g$ modulo $f$.

Where the analogy breaks: the vector-space picture remembers only addition and
scaling. A basis $1, \alpha, \dots, \alpha^{n-1}$ says nothing about how to
multiply two elements — that lives entirely in the minimal polynomial, and it is
the part you must carry separately.

## Concrete example

Build the field of eight elements as
$\mathbb{F}_8 = \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^3 + x + 1)$. The cubic is irreducible over
$\mathbb{F}_2$ because it has no root there ($0^3 + 0 + 1 = 1$,
$1^3 + 1 + 1 = 1$). Write $\alpha$ for the class of $x$, so $\alpha^3 = \alpha + 1$
(signs vanish in characteristic $2$). The eight elements are the polynomials in
$\alpha$ of degree at most $2$, and the nonzero seven are consecutive powers:

$$
\alpha^3 = \alpha + 1,\quad \alpha^4 = \alpha^2 + \alpha,\quad
\alpha^5 = \alpha^2 + \alpha + 1,\quad \alpha^6 = \alpha^2 + 1,\quad \alpha^7 = 1 .
$$

So $\mathbb{F}_8^\times$ is cyclic of order $7$, generated by $\alpha$. Multiply
$(\alpha^2 + 1)(\alpha^2 + \alpha)$ the slow way: expand to
$\alpha^4 + \alpha^3 + \alpha^2 + \alpha$, substitute, and the $\alpha^2$ terms
cancel, leaving $\alpha + 1$. The fast way: $\alpha^6 \cdot \alpha^4 =
\alpha^{10} = \alpha^3 = \alpha + 1$. Same answer.

In code, elements are $3$-bit integers, addition is XOR, and multiplication is
shift-and-reduce:

```python
MOD = 0b1011  # x^3 + x + 1

def mul(a, b):
    r = 0
    while b:
        if b & 1:
            r ^= a
        b >>= 1
        a <<= 1
        if a & 0b1000:
            a ^= MOD
    return r

assert mul(0b101, 0b110) == 0b011  # (a^2+1)(a^2+a) = a+1
```

## Formal treatment

Let $L/K$ be an extension. The **tower law** states that for $K \subseteq L
\subseteq M$ with finite degrees,

$$
[M:K] = [M:L]\,[L:K] ,
$$

proved by checking that products of the two bases form a basis of $M$ over $K$.

An element $\alpha \in L$ is **algebraic** over $K$ if $f(\alpha) = 0$ for some
nonzero $f \in K[x]$, and **transcendental** otherwise. For algebraic $\alpha$,
the set $\{f : f(\alpha) = 0\}$ is a nonzero ideal of the principal ideal domain
$K[x]$; its monic generator $m_\alpha$ is the **minimal polynomial**, it is
irreducible, and evaluation gives

$$
K(\alpha) \;\cong\; K[x]/(m_\alpha), \qquad [K(\alpha):K] = \deg m_\alpha ,
$$

with $K$-basis $1, \alpha, \dots, \alpha^{n-1}$. If $\alpha$ is transcendental,
$K(\alpha)$ is isomorphic to the rational function field $K(x)$ and the degree is
infinite. Read backwards, this is **Kronecker's construction**: given irreducible
$f \in K[x]$, the quotient $K[x]/(f)$ is a field in which $f$ has a root, built
from nothing but $K$.

Iterating it produces a **splitting field** of $f$: an extension in which $f$
factors into linear factors and which is generated over $K$ by the roots. It
exists for every $f$, has degree at most $(\deg f)!$, and is unique up to a
$K$-isomorphism — though not a canonical one.

The classification of finite fields follows. If $F$ is finite its characteristic
is some prime $p$, it contains $\mathbb{F}_p$ as prime subfield, and it is a
$\mathbb{F}_p$-vector space of some dimension $n$, so $|F| = p^n$: **every finite
field has prime power order**. Conversely, for each prime power $q = p^n$ there
is a field of order $q$, namely the splitting field of $x^q - x$ over
$\mathbb{F}_p$, and any two fields of order $q$ are isomorphic. Write it
$\mathbb{F}_q$. Further facts: $\mathbb{F}_q^\times$ is cyclic of order $q - 1$;
the Frobenius map $x \mapsto x^p$ is an automorphism fixing $\mathbb{F}_p$; and
$\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ contains exactly one copy of $\mathbb{F}_{p^d}$ for each
divisor $d \mid n$ and no other subfields.

## Assumptions and requirements

Commutativity is an axiom, not a convenience: drop it and you have a division
ring, where polynomials stop behaving — the quaternion equation $x^2 = -1$ has
infinitely many solutions.

$K[x]/(f)$ is a field **only** when $f$ is irreducible over $K$; for reducible
$f$ the quotient has zero divisors. Irreducibility is relative to $K$: $x^2 + 1$
is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$ and over $\mathbb{F}_3$, but over $\mathbb{F}_5$
it factors as $(x - 2)(x + 2)$. The finite-dimensional picture requires algebraic
elements, the tower law as an equation between integers requires finite degrees,
and building an algebraic closure of an arbitrary field uses Zorn's lemma.

Many of the theorems this page sets up also need **separability** — that the
minimal polynomial has no repeated roots in a splitting field. It is automatic in
characteristic $0$ and over finite fields, both of which are perfect, so it is
easy to forget it is a hypothesis. It fails over $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$: adjoining a
root of $x^p - t$ gives degree $p$ but only one root, hence one automorphism, and
the Galois correspondence does not apply.

## Uses and applicability

Reach for field theory when the question is "which numbers are reachable"
(constructibility, solvability by radicals, algebraic number theory), or when you
need exact, cheap arithmetic on a finite alphabet. Reed–Solomon codes are
evaluations of low-degree polynomials over $\mathbb{F}_q$, which bounds codeword
length by $q$ and is why CDs and QR codes work in $\mathbb{F}_{256}$; linear
codes generally are subspaces of $\mathbb{F}_q^n$, so generator and parity-check
matrices are ordinary linear algebra over a finite field. AES treats a byte as an
element of $\mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^8 + x^4 + x^3 + x + 1)$ and its S-box is inversion
there (with $0 \mapsto 0$) composed with an $\mathbb{F}_2$-affine map. Diffie–Hellman uses the cyclic group $\mathbb{F}_p^\times$. Computer
algebra systems reduce modulo a prime precisely to land where division is exact.

Do not reach for it when your structure has no inverses: the integers,
polynomial rings and matrix algebras are ring-theoretic objects, and forcing a
field on them loses the arithmetic you cared about. Floating-point numbers are
not a field either — addition is not associative and inverses are inexact, so
field identities need not survive numerically.

## Limitations and common mistakes

There is no field with $6$ elements, or $10$, or any order that is not a prime
power. Relatedly, $\mathbb{Z}/4$ is not $\mathbb{F}_4$: it is not a field at all,
since $2 \cdot 2 = 0$. The ring $\mathbb{Z}/p^n$ and the field $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$
agree only when $n = 1$.

"Unique up to isomorphism" is not "canonical". $\mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^3 + x + 1)$
and $\mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^3 + x^2 + 1)$ are isomorphic fields with different
multiplication tables, and code that mixes representations from two libraries
produces garbage that is hard to debug precisely because both are "the" field of
order $8$.

Adjoining one root is not splitting. $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ has degree $3$ and
sits inside $\mathbb{R}$, so it contains exactly one of the three cube roots of
$2$; the splitting field is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ with $\omega$ a
primitive cube root of unity, of degree $6$. That gap is what Galois theory is
built on.

Two more. The degree $[K(\alpha):K]$ is the degree of the _minimal_ polynomial,
not of whatever polynomial you happened to find vanishing at $\alpha$. And in
characteristic $p$ the identity $(a + b)^p = a^p + b^p$ is a theorem, not a
blunder, while dividing by $2$ is illegal in characteristic $2$ — which breaks
the quadratic formula and any argument that polarises a quadratic form.

One thing here is not a mathematical fact: the _hardness_ of discrete logarithms
in $\mathbb{F}_q^\times$. The group structure is completely known; the security
claim is computational, and it is weaker in small characteristic than in prime
fields, which is why deployed systems favour prime fields and elliptic curves.

## Variants and alternatives

**Division rings** (skew fields) drop commutativity — the quaternions are the
standard example — and Wedderburn's little theorem says you gain nothing finite
by it: every finite division ring is a field. **Ordered fields** carry a
compatible order, and **real closed fields** are their rigid version. **Valued
and complete fields** such as the $p$-adics $\mathbb{Q}_p$ or Laurent series
$\mathbb{F}_q((t))$ add a topology and support analysis. **Algebraically closed
fields** such as $\mathbb{C}$ and $\overline{\mathbb{F}_p}$ make every polynomial
split, buying uniform statements at the cost of infiniteness — no finite field is
algebraically closed. Number fields (finite extensions of $\mathbb{Q}$) and
function fields (finite extensions of $\mathbb{F}_q(t)$) behave so similarly that
results are routinely transported between them. Where inverses are genuinely
unavailable, the alternatives are rings and semirings.

## History and attribution

The subject has several strands rather than one founder. Galois, in the early
1830s, worked with what we would call fields while studying when an equation is
solvable by radicals, and finite fields of prime power order appear in his work —
they are still called Galois fields, and the notation $GF(q)$ survives. Dedekind
introduced the German term _Körper_ in the 1870s for subfields of $\mathbb{C}$
arising in algebraic number theory; the abstract axiomatic definition, free of
any ambient number system, is usually credited to Heinrich Weber in 1893, and
E. H. Moore proved around the same time that every finite field is a Galois
field. The systematic abstract theory — characteristic, prime field, algebraic
closure, separability, transcendence degree — was assembled by Ernst Steinitz in
a long 1910 paper, and that is essentially the shape taught today.

## Prerequisites and next connections

Read ring theory first: fields are commutative rings in which every nonzero
element is a unit, every construction above is a quotient of a polynomial ring,
and the language of ideals and irreducibility does the work.
[Vector Spaces](./vector-spaces.md) is equally load-bearing — degree _is_
dimension, and the tower law is a statement about bases.

What this opens up is Galois theory, which attaches a group to a splitting field
and turns the lattice of intermediate fields into a lattice of subgroups; the
insolvability of the general quintic is its headline consequence.
[Matrix Theory](./matrix-theory.md) and [Spectral Theory](./spectral-theory.md)
read differently once you see how much of their content depends on the underlying
field being algebraically closed, and [Complex Analysis](./complex-analysis.md)
is what happens when that closed field also carries a topology.
[Model Theory](./model-theory.md) takes fields as its main worked examples: the
theory of algebraically closed fields of fixed characteristic is complete.

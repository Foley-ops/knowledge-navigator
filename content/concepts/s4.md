## Definition

**S4** — the Structured State Space sequence model — is a neural network layer
that maps an input sequence $u_1, \dots, u_L$ to an output sequence of the same
length by simulating a linear, time-invariant state space model

$$
x'(t) = A x(t) + B u(t), \qquad y(t) = C x(t) + D u(t),
$$

with $x(t) \in \mathbb{C}^{N}$ a hidden state, $u$ and $y$ scalar signals, and
$A, B, C, D$ learned. Three specific choices make it S4 rather than a generic
state space layer: $A$ is **initialised to a HiPPO matrix**, it is
**parameterised as diagonal-plus-low-rank (DPLR)**, and training evaluates the
layer as a single length-$L$ convolution computed with an FFT rather than as a
step-by-step recurrence.

## Why it matters

Before S4, modelling dependencies over thousands of steps meant choosing between
bad options. A [Recurrent Neural Network](./recurrent-neural-networks.md) runs in
linear time but cannot be parallelised over the sequence and loses signal over
long horizons. Self-attention parallelises perfectly but costs time and memory
quadratic in the length. A linear state space model is unusual in being both:
because the dynamics do not depend on the input,
the whole layer collapses to one convolution, which trains in $O(L \log L)$, and
the same parameters run as an $O(1)$-per-step recurrence at generation time.

The catch was that forming that convolution kernel naively costs $O(N^2 L)$
operations and $O(NL)$ memory, because it needs $L$ powers of an $N \times N$
matrix. S4's structured parameterisation is what removed that cost, and that is
the whole engineering point of the paper.

## Intuition

Each S4 channel is a **linear filter**, in the sense of
[Fourier Analysis](./fourier-analysis.md) and classical signals theory: it has an
impulse response, and its output is the input convolved with it. A one-state
filter has an impulse response that is a single decaying exponential; $N$ states
give a sum of $N$ exponentials, possibly complex, hence damped oscillations. The
state $x(t)$ is a running, lossy summary of everything the channel has seen.

The HiPPO initialisation decides _which_ summary: it chooses $A$ and $B$ so the
state holds the coefficients of a polynomial (Legendre) approximation of the
input history under a chosen measure, making those $N$ numbers a principled
compression of the past rather than an arbitrary one. The filter-bank analogy is
honest as far as the linear algebra goes and breaks in one place: a classical
filter bank is designed by hand for a known signal class, whereas S4 starts from
the HiPPO basis and learns away from it.

## Concrete example

Take the smallest possible case: $N = 1$, $A = -0.5$, $B = C = 1$, step size
$\Delta = 1$. The bilinear discretisation gives

$$
\bar A = \frac{1 + \Delta A/2}{1 - \Delta A/2} = \frac{0.75}{1.25} = 0.6,
\qquad
\bar B = \frac{\Delta B}{1 - \Delta A/2} = \frac{1}{1.25} = 0.8,
$$

so the kernel is $\bar K_k = C \bar A^{k} \bar B = 0.8 \cdot 0.6^{k}$:
$0.8, 0.48, 0.288, 0.1728, \dots$ — an exponential with an effective horizon of
about $1/(1 - 0.6) = 2.5$ steps. Reaching a horizon of $10^4$ steps with one real
pole would need $\bar A \approx 1 - 10^{-4}$, the knife-edge that makes long-range
recurrence numerically awkward; HiPPO instead spreads many timescales across $N$
states rather than betting on one pole.

The two views really are the same computation:

```python
import numpy as np

A, B, C, dt, L = -0.5, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0, 8
Abar = (1 + dt / 2 * A) / (1 - dt / 2 * A)          # 0.6
Bbar = dt * B / (1 - dt / 2 * A)                    # 0.8
K = C * Bbar * Abar ** np.arange(L)                 # the SSM kernel

u = np.random.randn(L)
# FFT convolution, zero-padded to 2L so the circular wrap does not fold in
y_conv = np.fft.irfft(np.fft.rfft(K, 2 * L) * np.fft.rfft(u, 2 * L), 2 * L)[:L]

x, y_rec = 0.0, []
for k in range(L):
    x = Abar * x + Bbar * u[k]
    y_rec.append(C * x)

assert np.allclose(y_conv, y_rec)
```

## Formal treatment

Discretise with the bilinear transform at step $\Delta$:
$\bar A = (I - \tfrac{\Delta}{2} A)^{-1}(I + \tfrac{\Delta}{2} A)$ and
$\bar B = (I - \tfrac{\Delta}{2} A)^{-1} \Delta B$. The recurrence
$x_k = \bar A x_{k-1} + \bar B u_k$, $y_k = C x_k$ unrolls to a convolution
$y = \bar K * u$ with

$$
\bar K = \big(C \bar B,\; C \bar A \bar B,\; \dots,\; C \bar A^{L-1} \bar B\big)
\in \mathbb{C}^{L}.
$$

The HiPPO-LegS matrix used for initialisation is lower-triangular plus diagonal,

$$
A_{nk} = -\begin{cases}
(2n+1)^{1/2}(2k+1)^{1/2}, & n > k, \\
n + 1, & n = k, \\
0, & n < k,
\end{cases}
$$

and it is _not_ safely diagonalisable: the similarity transform that diagonalises
it has entries exponentially large in $N$, so the obvious trick of working in an
eigenbasis is numerically unusable. S4's fix is to show HiPPO matrices are
**normal plus low rank**, $A = V \Lambda V^{*} - PQ^{\top}$ with $V$ unitary and
the correction of rank one for LegS. Conjugating by $V$ leaves the
diagonal-plus-low-rank form $\Lambda - PQ^{*}$, which is stable to work with.

The kernel is then computed in the frequency domain rather than by matrix powers.
Its truncated generating function is
$\hat{K}(z) = \sum_{k<L} \bar K_k z^k = \tilde{C}(I - \bar A z)^{-1} \bar B$,
where $\tilde{C} = (I - \bar A^{L})^{*} C$ absorbs the truncation. Substituting
the bilinear $\bar A$ turns this into a resolvent of $A$ at
$\tfrac{2}{\Delta}\tfrac{1-z}{1+z}$; the Woodbury identity reduces the resolvent
of $\Lambda - PQ^{*}$ to the resolvent of $\Lambda$ plus rank-one corrections, so
each evaluation is a handful of **Cauchy kernels**

$$
k_{z,\Lambda}(v, w) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{v_i^{*} w_i}{z - \lambda_i}.
$$

Evaluating $\hat{K}$ at the $L$ roots of unity and taking an inverse FFT recovers
$\bar K$ in $\tilde{O}(N + L)$ operations and $O(N + L)$ memory, against
$O(N^2 L)$ and $O(NL)$ for the naive route. Applying the kernel is one more FFT
pair, $O(L \log L)$. A full layer stacks $H$ such single-input single-output
systems, one per feature channel, each with its own $\Delta$, $B$, $C$, and mixes
channels afterwards with a position-wise linear map and a nonlinearity.

## Assumptions and requirements

The convolutional view requires **time invariance**: $A, B, C, \Delta$ must not
depend on $u$ or on position. Drop that and there is no single kernel to FFT, and
the layer falls back to a sequential scan.

It also requires a **fixed or bounded length** at training time, since the kernel
is materialised to length $L$, and correct zero-padding, since an FFT convolution
is circular by default. Stability requires the discrete spectrum to satisfy
$|\lambda(\bar A)| \le 1$, which the bilinear transform guarantees when the
continuous eigenvalues have non-positive real part — but S4's parameterisation
does not enforce that during training, and the paper records that this version
can be numerically unstable when eigenvalues drift into the right half-plane,
which follow-up work fixed by changing the correction from $\Lambda - PQ^{*}$ to
$\Lambda - PP^{*}$. $\Delta$ is learned
in log space per channel, and a single shared $\Delta$ removes the spread of
timescales that makes the layer work. Finally the paper's long-range results
assume HiPPO initialisation: its ablation replacing $A$ with a random matrix
degrades accuracy sharply, with the same architecture and the same optimiser.

## Uses and applicability

Reach for S4 when the signal is long, roughly regular in time, and the structure
that matters is _when_ things happen rather than _which_ token matched which:
raw audio, sensor and biosignal streams, time series, pixel-by-pixel images. On
the Long Range Arena benchmark the original paper reported an
average around 86% and 96.4% on Path-X — a 16384-step task on which every model
tested before it sat at chance. That is a benchmark result on a suite of mostly
synthetic tasks, not a general statement that S4 beats attention on long inputs:
read it as evidence that the HiPPO-initialised kernel carries information across
very long horizons.

Do not reach for S4 when the task is content-based lookup — retrieval, copying a
span, in-context recall. A fixed filter cannot decide to attend to a particular
earlier token because the weights it applies do not depend on what it read.

## Limitations and common mistakes

The most common misreading is that S4's long memory comes from the FFT. It does
not. The FFT and the DPLR algebra are about _cost_; the long memory comes from
the HiPPO initialisation of $A$, and the paper's own ablations show random
initialisation performing far worse with everything else held fixed.

The second is treating S4 as a Transformer variant. There is no token-to-token
interaction inside an S4 layer: information moves along time through the state
and across channels through the position-wise mixing, never by comparing two
positions. The Mamba paper made the consequence explicit on synthetic selective
copying and induction-head tasks, where time-invariant SSMs fail and an
input-dependent one succeeds.

Third, the recurrent and convolutional forms are the same model but not the same
arithmetic: an FFT convolution and a long recurrent scan accumulate float error
differently, so small discrepancies between training-time and generation-time
outputs are expected, not a bug.

Fourth, the DPLR machinery is often assumed to be essential. Within a year,
diagonal-only models with careful initialisation recovered most of S4's quality,
which reframes DPLR as one way to keep HiPPO's spectrum rather than the reason
the model works. Implementations also need care: a naive Cauchy kernel costs
$O(NL)$ memory and erases the advantage, so it is usually a custom GPU kernel.

## Variants and alternatives

**S4D** and **DSS** replace the low-rank correction with a purely diagonal $A$,
initialised from the normal part of HiPPO; they buy a much simpler implementation
at a small, task-dependent accuracy cost. **Bidirectional S4** concatenates a
forward and a reverse kernel, giving non-causal context and forfeiting
autoregressive use. **Mamba** makes $B$, $C$ and $\Delta$ functions of the input, which restores
content-based selection and gives up the convolution, so it needs a
hardware-aware parallel scan instead. The genuinely different competitor
is self-attention, which pays $O(L^2)$ for weights that depend on content, with
linear attention and gated recurrent hybrids in between. The choice mostly turns
on whether the task needs selection or only memory.

## History and attribution

S4 was introduced by Albert Gu, Karan Goel and Christopher Ré in _Efficiently
Modeling Long Sequences with Structured State Spaces_ (2021 preprint, ICLR 2022).
It is the third step of a line from the same group: HiPPO supplied the matrices
that make a linear recurrence remember, and the Linear State-Space Layer showed
that one SSM is simultaneously a recurrence, a convolution and a continuous-time
model — but was too expensive to train, since it needed those matrix powers. The
problem S4 solved was therefore computational, not conceptual: make known-good
HiPPO dynamics cheap enough to stack into a deep network. The underlying state space formalism is far older, standard in
control theory and signal processing since the 1960s; S4's contribution is the
structured parameterisation and the kernel algorithm, not the model class.

## Sources

Gu, Goel and Ré's paper is the source for everything specific here: the layer
definition, the bilinear discretisation, the NPLR/DPLR decomposition, the
generating-function and Cauchy-kernel algorithm, the HiPPO ablation and the Long
Range Arena numbers. MIT 6.003 is background for the intuition only — linear
time-invariant systems, impulse responses, convolution and transforms — and
contains nothing about S4. The Mamba paper is cited for the diagnosis that
time-invariant SSMs fail at content-based selection. Vaswani et al. is cited only
for the attention alternative and its quadratic per-layer cost.

## Definition

Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $k$ and let $Q : V \to k$ be a quadratic
form. The **Clifford algebra** $\mathrm{Cl}(V,Q)$ is the associative unital
$k$-algebra generated by $V$ subject to the single family of relations

$$
v^2 = Q(v)\,1 \qquad \text{for every } v \in V,
$$

and to nothing else. "Nothing else" is made precise by a quotient: if
$T(V) = \bigoplus_{m \ge 0} V^{\otimes m}$ is the tensor algebra on $V$ and
$I_Q$ is the two-sided ideal generated by all $v \otimes v - Q(v)1$, then
$\mathrm{Cl}(V,Q) = T(V)/I_Q$. The composite
$V \hookrightarrow T(V) \twoheadrightarrow \mathrm{Cl}(V,Q)$ is injective, so
$V$ sits inside its own Clifford algebra. If $\dim V = n$ then
$\dim_k \mathrm{Cl}(V,Q) = 2^n$, independent of $Q$; the product is called the
**geometric product** when the emphasis is geometric.

## Why it matters

One relation buys three things at once.

It **manufactures the classical number systems.** Take $V = \mathbb{R}$ with
$Q(x) = -x^2$ and you get $\mathbb{R}[e]/(e^2+1) \cong \mathbb{C}$; take the
negative-definite plane and you get the quaternions. Nothing in the construction
knows it is supposed to produce them.

It **turns orthogonal geometry into multiplication.** Rotations become
conjugation by invertible even elements, $v \mapsto x v x^{-1}$, and reflections
the twisted conjugation $v \mapsto \alpha(x) v x^{-1}$ built from the grading
involution $\alpha$ below, so composing them is multiplying. That is where the
unit-quaternion representation of 3D rotation comes from, and the Clifford
picture says how far it generalises.

It **produces spinors**, objects transforming under the double cover
$\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ and not under $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ itself — behaviour no tensor
power of $V$ exhibits. Dirac needed a square root of the wave operator, and the
gamma matrices that provide it satisfy exactly the Clifford relation for the
Minkowski form.

## Intuition

Fix a $Q$-orthogonal basis $e_1,\dots,e_n$. The defining relation then says two
things: each $e_i$ squares to a scalar, and distinct basis vectors
anticommute, $e_ie_j = -e_je_i$. Multiplication is therefore bookkeeping. Any
product of basis vectors can be sorted into increasing order at the cost of a
sign, and any repeated factor collapses to a scalar. What survives is a subset
of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ — hence $2^n$ basis elements, indexed by subsets.

Those basis elements have geometric readings: $1$ is a scalar, $e_i$ a
direction, $e_ie_j$ the oriented plane they span, $e_1\cdots e_n$ the oriented
volume — and the algebra lets you multiply them together.

The useful analogy is the exterior algebra $\Lambda V$, where $v \wedge v = 0$:
the Clifford algebra is the same bookkeeping with $v^2 = Q(v)$ instead of $0$,
the exterior algebra with a metric switched on. The analogy breaks at grading.
In $\Lambda V$ a $1$-vector times a $2$-vector is a $3$-vector; in
$\mathrm{Cl}(V,Q)$ it is a $1$-vector plus a $3$-vector. Clifford algebra is only
**filtered** over $\mathbb{Z}$, not graded; only a $\mathbb{Z}/2$ grading by
parity survives.

## Concrete example

**The quaternions.** Take $V = \mathbb{R}^2$ and $Q(x) = -(x_1^2+x_2^2)$, so
$e_1^2 = e_2^2 = -1$. The algebra $\mathrm{Cl}_{0,2}(\mathbb{R})$ has basis
$\{1, e_1, e_2, e_1e_2\}$, and

$$
(e_1e_2)^2 = e_1e_2e_1e_2 = -e_1e_1e_2e_2 = -(-1)(-1) = -1 .
$$

So all three of $e_1, e_2, e_1e_2$ square to $-1$ and pairwise anticommute:
these are $i$, $j$, $k$, and $\mathrm{Cl}_{0,2}(\mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{H}$.

**A rotation by sandwiching.** Work in $\mathrm{Cl}_{3,0}(\mathbb{R})$, where
$e_i^2 = +1$. Put $R = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}(1 - e_1e_2)$, whose inverse is
$\tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}(1 + e_1e_2)$. Then

$$
R\,e_1\,R^{-1} = \tfrac12 (1 - e_1e_2)\,e_1\,(1 + e_1e_2)
= \tfrac12 (e_1 + e_2)(1 + e_1e_2) = \tfrac12 (2e_2) = e_2 ,
$$

using $e_1e_2e_1 = -e_2$ and $e_2e_1e_2 = -e_1$. Meanwhile $e_3$ commutes with
$e_1e_2$, so $R e_3 R^{-1} = e_3$. The element $R$ implements the $90^\circ$
rotation in the $e_1e_2$ plane. In general
$R = \exp(-\tfrac{\theta}{2}e_1e_2) = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2} - \sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,e_1e_2$
rotates by $\theta$; note the half-angle, and note that $R$ and $-R$ give the
same rotation. That two-to-one redundancy is the spin double cover, visible in
four lines of algebra.

## Formal treatment

$\mathrm{Cl}(V,Q)$ is characterised by a universal property: for any unital
associative $k$-algebra $A$ and any linear $f : V \to A$ with
$f(v)^2 = Q(v)1_A$, there is a unique algebra homomorphism
$\tilde f : \mathrm{Cl}(V,Q) \to A$ with $\tilde f|_V = f$. Everything below
follows from this and from the quotient description.

**Polarisation.** Expanding $(u+v)^2 = Q(u+v)$ gives

$$
uv + vu = 2B(u,v), \qquad B(u,v) = \tfrac12\big(Q(u+v)-Q(u)-Q(v)\big),
$$

so orthogonal vectors anticommute and $B$ is recoverable from the product.

**Basis and dimension.** With a $Q$-orthogonal basis $e_1,\dots,e_n$, the
products $e_{i_1}e_{i_2}\cdots e_{i_m}$ for $i_1 < \cdots < i_m$ form a basis,
giving $\dim = 2^n$.

**Grading.** The map $v \mapsto -v$ extends to an involution $\alpha$, splitting
$\mathrm{Cl} = \mathrm{Cl}^0 \oplus \mathrm{Cl}^1$ into even and odd parts. The
**even subalgebra** $\mathrm{Cl}^0$ has dimension $2^{n-1}$, and in the real
case $\mathrm{Cl}^0_{p,q} \cong \mathrm{Cl}_{p,q-1} \cong \mathrm{Cl}_{q,p-1}$.
For instance $\mathrm{Cl}^0_{0,2} = \mathrm{span}\{1,e_1e_2\} \cong \mathbb{C}$
sits inside $\mathbb{H}$.

**Groups.** For a unit vector $u$ with $Q(u)=1$, the twisted conjugation
$v \mapsto \alpha(u)vu^{-1} = -uvu^{-1}$ fixes $u^\perp$ pointwise and sends
$u \mapsto -u$: it is reflection in the hyperplane $u^\perp$. Since every
orthogonal transformation is a product of reflections, the group generated by
unit vectors surjects onto $\mathrm{O}(V,Q)$. Restricting to products of an even
number of unit vectors gives $\mathrm{Spin}(V,Q) \subset \mathrm{Cl}^0$, and

$$
1 \to \{\pm 1\} \to \mathrm{Spin}(n) \to \mathrm{SO}(n) \to 1
$$

is a double cover: $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is connected for $n \ge 2$ and simply
connected for $n \ge 3$, so it is the universal cover of $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ from
dimension three up. The
bivectors $e_ie_j$ span a Lie algebra under commutator isomorphic to
$\mathfrak{so}(n)$, which is why rotors are exponentials of bivectors. Low
dimensions give the exceptional isomorphisms $\mathrm{Spin}(3) \cong
\mathrm{SU}(2)$, $\mathrm{Spin}(4) \cong \mathrm{SU}(2)\times\mathrm{SU}(2)$,
$\mathrm{Spin}(5) \cong \mathrm{Sp}(2)$, $\mathrm{Spin}(6) \cong \mathrm{SU}(4)$.

**Classification.** Over $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathrm{Cl}_n$ is $M_{2^{n/2}}(\mathbb{C})$
for even $n$ and a sum of two such blocks for odd $n$: period $2$. Over
$\mathbb{R}$ the period is $8$, with
$\mathrm{Cl}_{p+8,q} \cong \mathrm{Cl}_{p,q}\otimes M_{16}(\mathbb{R})$ and
$\mathrm{Cl}_{p+1,q+1} \cong \mathrm{Cl}_{p,q}\otimes M_2(\mathbb{R})$. Every
$\mathrm{Cl}_{p,q}(\mathbb{R})$ is a matrix algebra over $\mathbb{R}$,
$\mathbb{C}$ or $\mathbb{H}$, or a sum of two of them, and the irreducible
modules of those algebras are the spinor representations.

## Assumptions and requirements

Everything above assumes $V$ is **finite-dimensional**; $\dim = 2^n$ is
meaningless otherwise, though the construction survives (the canonical
anticommutation relation algebra of fermionic field theory is an
infinite-dimensional instance).

Orthogonal bases require $\operatorname{char} k \neq 2$. In characteristic $2$
the polarisation identity loses its factor of $2$ and $Q$ cannot be recovered
from $B$, so the algebra must be defined from the quadratic form, as the quotient
above does — which is why treatments that want to cover characteristic $2$, and
formal libraries, which work over a general commutative ring, start from $Q$
rather than from a symmetric bilinear form.

$Q$ may be degenerate; $Q = 0$ gives the exterior algebra. The group theory,
however, needs $Q$ **nondegenerate**: a vector $v$ with $Q(v) \neq 0$ is always
invertible, $v^{-1} = Q(v)^{-1}v$, degenerate form or not, but only for
nondegenerate $Q$ does the twisted adjoint map reach all of $\mathrm{O}(V,Q)$
and only then is the algebra semisimple. Simple connectivity of
$\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ needs $n \ge 3$: $\mathrm{Spin}(2) \cong \mathrm{U}(1)$
double covers $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ but has fundamental group $\mathbb{Z}$.

## Uses and applicability

Reach for Clifford algebra when a problem carries a quadratic form and you want
rotations, reflections or square roots of second-order operators expressed
algebraically: spinors and the Dirac operator; rotation representation in
graphics and robotics, where rotors generalise quaternions to any dimension and
signature; K-theory, where the mod-$8$ periodicity of real Clifford algebras is
the algebraic shadow of Bott periodicity; the classification of quadratic forms,
where the algebra's Brauer class is an invariant; and fermionic quantum systems,
whose creation and annihilation operators satisfy Clifford relations.

Do not reach for it when the geometry is affine or projective with no metric —
the exterior algebra suffices — or when you only need 3D rotations and a
quaternion library exists. The $2^n$ growth also makes dense representation
impractical past about $n = 10$.

## Limitations and common mistakes

**Signature order matters.** $\mathrm{Cl}_{2,0}(\mathbb{R}) \cong
M_2(\mathbb{R})$ while $\mathrm{Cl}_{0,2}(\mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{H}$: same
dimension, non-isomorphic algebras. Worse, the literature is split between
$v^2 = Q(v)$ and $v^2 = -Q(v)$, so $\mathrm{Cl}_{1,3}$ in one book is
$\mathrm{Cl}_{3,1}$ in another — and those two are genuinely different
($M_2(\mathbb{H})$ versus $M_4(\mathbb{R})$). Always read the convention first.

**It is not the exterior algebra.** There is a canonical _linear_ isomorphism
$\mathrm{Cl}(V,Q) \cong \Lambda V$, and it tempts people into treating Clifford
products as graded. They are not: the identity $uv = B(u,v) + u\wedge v$ holds
for two _vectors_, not for general multivectors.

**Spinors are not tensors.** $\mathrm{Spin}(n) \to \mathrm{SO}(n)$ is
two-to-one, and the spin representation does not descend to $\mathrm{SO}(n)$.
Rotating a spinor through $2\pi$ multiplies it by $-1$; $4\pi$ is needed to
return. A "square root of a vector representation" cannot be built by tensor
operations on $V$.

**Name collisions.** The Clifford group of quantum computing — the normaliser of
the Pauli group — is not the Clifford–Lipschitz group described here; and there
are two inequivalent $\mathrm{Pin}$ groups, $\mathrm{Pin}^+$ and
$\mathrm{Pin}^-$, one per sign convention, even though $\mathrm{Spin}$ is
unambiguous.

## Variants and alternatives

**Geometric algebra** is the same mathematics: the real Clifford algebra, with
notation and vocabulary aimed at computation (rotors, blades, the geometric
product) rather than classification. Treating the two as different subjects is a
mistake of sociology, not of mathematics. Within it, **conformal geometric
algebra** uses $\mathrm{Cl}_{4,1}$ so that translations as well as rotations
become conjugations, and **projective geometric algebra** deliberately adds a
degenerate direction.

**Exterior algebra** is the $Q = 0$ degenerate case — cheaper, no metric.
**Weyl algebras** are the symplectic counterpart, imposing $uv - vu = \omega(u,v)$
instead; the two are the fermionic and bosonic quantisations of one idea.
**Complex Clifford algebras** $\mathrm{Cl}(V,Q)\otimes\mathbb{C}$ trade the
eight-fold real periodicity for a two-fold one, forgetting signature.
**Clifford algebras of modules over a commutative ring** are the general setting
used in formalised mathematics. For rotations specifically the competitors are
rotation matrices and Euler angles (gimbal lock); quaternions are not a
competitor but a Clifford algebra under another name, and the octonions, being
nonassociative, cannot be one at all.

## History and attribution

Two systems came first: Hamilton's quaternions (1843) and Grassmann's exterior
algebra (1844). William Kingdon Clifford combined them in 1878, in a paper
applying Grassmann's calculus, and named the resulting systems _geometric
algebras_ — the modern revival of that phrase is a restoration, not a coinage.
Clifford died the following year, aged 33. Rudolf Lipschitz arrived at the same
algebras independently and was the first to use them to represent rotations of
Euclidean space, introducing both the group of products of vectors that still
carries his name and the spin groups themselves.

The objects were rediscovered from the physics side: Élie Cartan discovered
spinors in 1913 in his work on the linear representations of simple groups —
and later stressed their connection with the Clifford–Lipschitz hypercomplex
numbers — while Dirac's 1928 gamma matrices are a Clifford algebra of Minkowski
space, though Dirac was after a first-order wave equation rather than an
algebra. Brauer and Weyl systematised spinors in $n$ dimensions in the 1930s;
Chevalley's _The Algebraic Theory of Spinors_ (1954) gave the basis-free
treatment, new at the time and valid over any field;
Atiyah, Bott and Shapiro connected Clifford modules to K-theory in 1964. David
Hestenes revived the geometric reading from 1966 onwards. The subject genuinely
has several independent origins, which is why its terminology is still not
standardised.

same quotient and universal property, identifies $\mathrm{Cl}_2$ with the

reflection and that the Clifford group therefore surjects onto
$\mathrm{O}(V)$, proves $\mathrm{Spin}(V) \to \mathrm{SO}(V)$ is a double cover
that is connected in dimension $\ge 2$ and simply connected in dimension
$\ge 3$, identifies the Lie algebra of $\mathrm{Spin}(V)$ with the bivectors
under commutator, and classifies the complex Clifford modules as one or two
matrix blocks. MacTutor's biographies of Clifford, Lipschitz, Cartan, Chevalley,
Hamilton and Grassmann carry the attributions in the history below.

What remains uncited is the physics — gamma matrices and the Dirac equation —
the conformal and projective geometric algebras used in graphics and robotics,
and the eight-fold periodicity of the real Clifford algebras with its reading as
Bott periodicity; those sit in the unresolved references.
